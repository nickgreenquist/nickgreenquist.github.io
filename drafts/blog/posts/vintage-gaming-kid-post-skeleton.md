## 1. The issue
*(2-4 sentences. Open here — this is the gripe.)*

- Every device aimed at kids has internet, a store, and ads.
- Modern kids' games aren't just annoying, they're engineered against the kid.
- Line to land: the games are designed to serve the company, not the child.

**Research to cite (pick 2, don't stack them):**
- 95% of apps for children ages 5 and under contain at least one form of advertising; in some cases ad exposure surpassed time spent actually playing the game. (Radesky et al., U. Michigan)
- 80% of the most popular preschool apps contain at least one manipulative feature — "dark patterns" like crying characters or hidden exit buttons.
- Kids under 13 often can't recognize persuasive intent in advertising at all. They're not ignoring the sales pitch; they can't see it.
- Ads that can't be dismissed for several seconds, then a tiny X — miss it and you're in the advertiser's site.
- Worth one line: these tricks show up *disproportionately* in apps used by kids from lower-income households.

## 2. The question
*(1-2 sentences. Short.)*

- Is there a way to give a very young kid a gaming experience from before any of this existed?
- Not "less screen time." A different *kind* of screen.

## 3. The solution
*(3-5 sentences. Keep the hardware brief — this is not a build log.)*

- A $35 Linux handheld (R36S), ArkOS, one SD card.
- No wifi hardware. No store. No ads. Nothing to install.
- The whitelist is literally which files are on the card. I control it completely.
- One game at a time: Pokémon Red first. Silver and Emerald later, when I decide.
- **Do NOT write up the Etcher failure / DTB rabbit hole here.** Different post.

## 4. The compromise
*(2-4 sentences. This is the honest section — don't skip it.)*

- It's not real vintage hardware. It's emulation on a cheap Chinese board.
- No cartridges, no authentic ritual. FPGA options exist (Analogue Pocket, ModRetro Chromatic) at 6x the price.
- $35 + an SD card, and the experience is right even if the hardware isn't.
- Good enough beat perfect here.

## 5. Pros
*(bullet list, short)*

- No internet, no store, no ads, no IAP — not blocked, just absent
- I choose every game that goes on it
- Physical buttons instead of a touchscreen
- Games that let you lose — failure is part of the loop, not a paywall
- Replaceable: hardware is $35, the SD card is the real thing
- Cheap enough that I don't care if a kid drops it

## 6. Cons
*(be honest — this is what makes it credible)*

- Retro games are genuinely hard by modern standards
- Setup is fiddly (understatement — see the other post)
- Cheap hardware, no manufacturer, no support
- Not authentic hardware, so purists will scoff
- He'll eventually want what his friends have, and this won't be it
- **The big one: he's 1. I have tested none of this on an actual child.**

## 7. The point
*(2-3 sentences. Land it, don't dwell.)*

- The filter isn't "easy games." It's games where failure costs almost nothing.
- Hard isn't the enemy. Punishing is.
- Ask me in four years whether it worked.
